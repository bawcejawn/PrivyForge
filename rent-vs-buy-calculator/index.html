How to use the rent vs buy calculator

  1. Describe the home. Enter the price, down payment, mortgage rate and term, then property tax, insurance, maintenance and any HOA fee.
  2. Describe the rental. Enter the rent for a comparable place and how fast you expect rents to rise.
  3. Set your assumptions. Home appreciation and investment return drive the result more than anything else, so try a few values.
  4. Choose how many years to compare. Match it to how long you realistically expect to stay.
  5. Read the verdict and chart. The two lines show your net worth on each path, year by year.

Worked example: a $450,000 home vs $2,400 rent

With the default inputs (20% down, 6.5% for 30 years, 1.1% property tax, $1,800 insurance, 1% maintenance, 3.5% appreciation, rent rising 3% a year, 6% investment return), the calculator shows:

  • Mortgage principal and interest: $2,275.44 a month.
  • First-month cost to own: $3,238, against $2,415 to rent (rent plus renter's insurance).
  • After year 1: the buyer's net worth is $81,829 and the renter's is $119,699. Buying starts behind because closing costs, and the cost of selling, are counted from day one.
  • Buying comes out ahead after 10 years. After 15 years the buyer has $447,458 and the renter $397,127, so buying leaves you $50,331 wealthier.

Now change one assumption at a time. With home appreciation at 2% instead of 3.5%, renting stays ahead for all 15 years, by $61,871 at the end. With the investment return at 4% instead of 6%, buying wins after just 6 years. Small changes in these two inputs can flip the answer, so test the range you think is realistic.

What this calculator counts

  • Owning: mortgage payment, property tax, insurance, maintenance, HOA, about 3% closing costs to buy and 6% to sell, and home appreciation.
  • Renting: rent rising each year and renter's insurance. The renter invests the down payment and closing costs on day one.
  • The difference: each month, whichever option is cheaper invests the savings at your chosen return. That's what makes the comparison fair.

Buying usually loses in the first few years, because closing and selling costs are steep, and gains over time as the loan is paid down and the home appreciates. The faster rents rise, and the longer you stay, the better buying looks.

Frequently asked questions

Why does buying look so bad in the first year?

The buyer's net worth assumes the home is sold at that point, so it subtracts selling costs, and closing costs to buy are already spent. Those costs are only spread thin if you stay for years.

Does it include tax deductions?

No. Since the 2018 tax changes most homeowners take the standard deduction, so the mortgage-interest deduction often doesn't apply. If you itemize, buying is slightly better than shown.

What return should I use?

Many people use 5–7% for a diversified stock portfolio over long periods, but returns aren't guaranteed. Try a lower number to stress-test your decision.

What if I wouldn't actually invest the difference?

Then renting looks better here than it would be for you. The comparison is only fair if the renter really invests the down payment and any monthly savings.

Is this financial advice?

No. It's a model; real outcomes depend on your market and choices.
