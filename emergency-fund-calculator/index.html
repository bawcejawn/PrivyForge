How to use the emergency fund calculator

  1. Enter your essential monthly expenses — what you'd have to keep paying if your income stopped: housing, utilities, groceries, insurance, transport and minimum debt payments. Tick "Add them up for me" to enter them one by one. If you've filled in the budget calculator, your Needs total is a good number to use.
  2. Enter what you've saved for emergencies so far, and how much you can add each month.
  3. Choose a target: 3 months, 6 months, or a custom number.
  4. Optionally add your savings account's APY to include interest. Leave it blank to see the plan on deposits alone.

The result shows the target amount, how many months your current savings would cover, the gap, and how long it takes to close it. The chart and table show your balance month by month. Your inputs are saved in the page link, so you can bookmark it or save it on My PrivyForge.

How big should an emergency fund be?

Common guidance is to keep 3 to 6 months of essential expenses somewhere safe and easy to reach. It's based on essential spending, not income, because in an emergency you'd cut the extras. Many people aim higher — 6 months or more — when their income is irregular (freelance, commission, seasonal work), when one paycheck supports the whole household, or when their job would take a long time to replace. A smaller first goal, like one month of expenses, is a sensible milestone on the way.

Worked example

Say your essential expenses are $3,000 a month and you're aiming for 6 months. Your target is $3,000 × 6 = $18,000.

  • You have $4,000 saved, which covers 1.3 months of expenses. The gap is $14,000.
  • Saving $400 a month with no interest, you close the gap in $14,000 ÷ $400 = 35 months (2 yr 11 mo).
  • Put the money in an account paying 4% APY and it takes 33 months (2 yr 9 mo): you deposit $13,200 and earn $1,170.95 of interest, finishing at $18,370.95.

If 6 months feels far away, switch the target to 3 months: $9,000, a $5,000 gap, and 13 months at $400 a month without interest.

How the calculator works

The target is your essential monthly expenses times the number of months. "Months covered" is your current savings divided by monthly expenses, rounded down to one decimal so it never overstates your cushion. Each month, interest (if you entered an APY) is added at the monthly equivalent of that yield — the same method as the compound interest calculator — rounded to the cent, and then your monthly deposit is added. The plan ends in the first month the balance reaches the target. Savings rates are variable, so treat the interest figure as an estimate.

Where to keep it matters too: an emergency fund needs to be available quickly and shouldn't swing in value, which is why many people use a savings account rather than investments.

Frequently asked questions

Should I count rent and debt payments?

Yes. Count anything you'd still have to pay if your income stopped: rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, insurance, transport and the minimum payments on your debts. Leave out things you'd cut, such as eating out or extra debt payments.

3 months or 6 months?

Common guidance is 3 to 6 months. The steadier your income and the more earners in your household, the closer to 3 you might go; irregular income, a single paycheck or a job that's hard to replace are reasons to aim for 6 or more. Use "Custom" for any other number.

Should I build an emergency fund before paying off debt?

Many people do both: build a small starter cushion first so a surprise bill doesn't go on a credit card, then split extra money between the fund and high-interest debt. The budget calculator helps you see how much you can put toward each.

Does the interest make much difference?

Some, over a long build-up. In the example above, 4% APY shortens the plan from 35 to 33 months. The amount you can save each month matters much more.

Is my data private?

Yes. The calculation runs in your browser and nothing you type is sent anywhere. Your inputs are kept only in the page link.

Is this financial advice?

No. It's a calculator based on common guidance. Check account terms and current rates with your bank, and talk to a qualified professional about your situation.
