    How to use the budget calculator

    1. Enter your monthly take-home pay — what lands in your account after tax and payroll deductions. If you're paid every two weeks, multiply one paycheck by 26 and divide by 12.
    2. Replace the sample lines with your own amounts. Rename any line, remove it with ✕, or add more with the "+ Add" buttons. Use monthly amounts: divide a yearly bill by 12.
    3. Pick a guideline. 50/30/20 is the default. Switch to 60/20/20 or 70/20/10, or type your own percentages.
    4. Read the result. The bars show your share of take-home pay for each group, with a tick at the guideline. The notes spell out the gaps in dollars, and the top line shows what's left over or how much you're overspending.

    Everything you type is kept in the page's link, so you can bookmark it or save it as a scenario on My PrivyForge and come back to it next month.

    What the 50/30/20 rule says

    The 50/30/20 rule is a simple way to split take-home pay into three buckets:

    • 50% for needs — the things you must pay to live and work: rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, insurance, transport and the minimum payments on your debts.
    • 30% for wants — everything you could cut without real hardship: restaurants, streaming and other subscriptions, travel, shopping beyond the basics.
    • 20% for savings and debt — building savings and paying down debt faster than the minimum.

    It's a rule of thumb, not a law. Where housing is expensive, needs can easily take more than half of take-home pay, which is why some people use 60/20/20 or 70/20/10 instead. The useful part is less the exact numbers and more the habit of checking where the money goes and deciding on purpose.

    Worked example

    The sample budget assumes $5,000 a month of take-home pay. Needs add up to $2,950 (rent $1,600, utilities $180, groceries $450, insurance $220, transport $350, minimum debt payments $150). Wants are $635 (dining out $300, subscriptions $85, shopping $250). Savings and extra debt payments are $600.

    Against 50/30/20 the calculator reports:

    • Needs are 59% of take-home; the guideline is 50%. That's $450 a month over.
    • Wants are 12.7%; the guideline is 30% — $865 under.
    • Savings & debt are 12%; the guideline is 20% — $400 under.
    • $815 a month isn't assigned to anything. Sending it to savings would bring Savings & debt to 28.3%.

    Switch the guideline to 60/20/20 and needs are now $50 under the guideline, which may be a fairer target in a high-rent city. Now raise rent to $2,200, dining out to $500 and shopping to $600: needs jump to 71%, wants to 23.7%, and the calculator warns that you're spending $335 a month more than you take home.

    Needs or wants? Some judgement calls

    Groceries are a need; the premium brand might be a want. A car payment you can't get out of is a need; the upgrade you're considering is a want. Minimum payments on debt are needs, because missing them has real consequences — anything above the minimum counts as savings and debt, because it's a choice that improves your future. When you're unsure, ask: would I still pay this if I lost my job tomorrow? If yes, it's probably a need.

    The calculator adds amounts to the cent and shows percentages to one decimal place. Once your needs are known, the emergency fund calculator can use them to size a safety cushion, and the compound interest calculator shows what your monthly savings could grow to.

    Frequently asked questions

    Should I use take-home pay or gross salary?

    Take-home pay — the amount that actually reaches your bank account after tax and payroll deductions. The 50/30/20 rule is usually described in terms of after-tax income. If you have retirement contributions taken from your paycheck, you can add them back as a savings line and to your take-home pay so they count.

    What if my needs are well over 50%?

    That's common where rent is high. Try the 60/20/20 or 70/20/10 guideline, look for the biggest need you can realistically lower, and check the wants column for money to move. The point is to see the trade-offs clearly, not to hit a number exactly.

    Where do debt payments go?

    Minimum payments are needs. Anything you pay above the minimum goes under savings and debt, because it's money you're choosing to put toward your future.

    Is my budget stored anywhere?

    Only in this page's link in your browser, so you can bookmark it. Nothing you type is sent to a server. If you save it as a scenario, it's kept in your browser's local storage on this device.

    Is this financial advice?

    No. It's a calculator that compares your numbers with a common rule of thumb. For advice about your situation, talk to a qualified professional.
