Leases, job offers, school forms, medical consents: the documents you're asked to sign are often the ones with your address, date of birth and account numbers on them. Most online signing services upload the file to their servers to work on it. You don't need to.

## Sign a PDF in your browser, step by step

1. Open [Sign PDF](/sign-pdf/) and drop in your document. The pages appear below each other.
2. Click **Signature** and create it: draw it with your mouse, finger or stylus; type your name and pick a style; or upload a photo of your signature on white paper (the background is removed automatically).
3. Click the spot on the page where it should go. Drag to move it and pull the corner to resize.
4. Add **initials**, today's **date**, **text** (your printed name, for example) and **checkmarks** wherever the document needs them.
5. Click **Download signed PDF**. You get a new copy named *yourfile-signed.pdf*; your original is untouched.

Everything happens on your device: the PDF is read into your browser's memory and the signed copy is written back to your disk. You can check this yourself by loading the page, turning off Wi-Fi, and signing a document.

If the PDF has **fillable form fields** (boxes you can click and type into), fill them first with [Fill PDF Form](/fill-pdf-form/), then sign the result.

## What kind of signature is this?

It's a **simple electronic signature**: an image of your signature placed on the page, along with any text or dates you add. That's what most everyday signing involves, and it's what people usually mean by "sign this and send it back".

It is **not** a certificate-based *digital signature*, the kind that cryptographically locks a document and proves who signed it. Some government, legal and corporate processes require that kind, usually through a specific provider. If the sender asks for a "digital signature" by name, or sends you a link to a signing service, use what they ask for.

## Are electronic signatures legally valid?

In the United States, the federal **ESIGN Act** and state laws based on the **Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA)** generally give electronic signatures the same legal effect as handwritten ones, when the parties agree to do business electronically. There are exceptions, such as wills and certain family-law and court documents, and other countries have their own rules.

In practice, whether your signed PDF is accepted is up to the person or organization you're sending it to. *This isn't legal advice. If a document matters a lot, check what the recipient accepts.*

## Tips for a clean result

- **Sign once, reuse it.** Tick "Remember my signature on this device" to keep it in this browser only. It's off by default, and unticking it deletes the saved copy.
- **Photographed signature?** Sign with a dark pen on plain white paper, in good light, and crop the photo close to the ink.
- **Check every page.** Contracts often want initials on each page. Use Initials and click through the pages.
- **Keep the original.** Save the unsigned PDF alongside the signed one in case you need to redo it.

## Common questions

**Is it free? Do I need an account?**
Yes, it's free, and no account is needed. There are no limits on pages or documents.

**Can the recipient edit my signature out?**
A simple electronic signature is part of the page content, and anyone with PDF editing software could alter a PDF. That's true of most everyday signed PDFs. If tamper-evidence matters, the parties should use a certificate-based signing service.

**Does signing change the rest of the document?**
No. The original text stays selectable and searchable; your signature, dates and text are added on top.

**Can I sign on my phone?**
Yes. Draw your signature with your finger, then tap the page to place it.

*Not legal advice. Laws on electronic signatures vary by country and by type of document.*
