If you have more than one debt and some money left over after the minimum payments, the question is simple: **which debt gets the extra money first?** The two best-known answers are the *avalanche* and the *snowball*. Both work. They differ in what they optimize — dollars or motivation.

## The two methods in one sentence each

- **Avalanche:** pay the minimum on everything, then put every spare dollar on the debt with the **highest interest rate**. When it's gone, roll that whole payment onto the next-highest rate.
- **Snowball:** pay the minimum on everything, then put every spare dollar on the debt with the **smallest balance**. When it's gone, roll that payment onto the next-smallest balance.

In both cases the payment "rolls over": when a debt is paid off, the money you were paying toward it doesn't go back into your budget — it joins the attack on the next debt. That rollover is what makes either method much faster than paying minimums.

## A worked example with real numbers

Here are three debts, a total of $22,500. The minimum payments add up to $615 a month, and this household can put **$900 a month** toward debt.

| Debt | Balance | APR | Minimum |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Personal loan | $2,500 | 11% | $85 |
| Credit card | $8,000 | 24% | $240 |
| Car loan | $12,000 | 6.5% | $290 |

We ran these numbers through the [PrivyForge Debt Payoff Planner](/debt-payoff-calculator/), which adds interest monthly (rounded to the cent) and rolls each paid-off minimum onto the next target.

| Strategy | Debt-free in | Total interest | Pays off first |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Avalanche | 29 months | $3,238.83 | Credit card (month 19) |
| Snowball | 30 months | $3,613.89 | Personal loan (month 8) |
| Minimum payments only | 56 months | $7,365.84 | — |

What this shows:

1. **Both methods beat minimums by a mile.** Either one cuts more than two years and roughly $4,000 of interest compared with paying only the minimums.
2. **Avalanche is cheaper** — here by $375.06 and one month — because the 24% credit card stops growing sooner.
3. **Snowball wins on momentum.** The personal loan disappears in month 8, eleven months before the avalanche clears its first debt. For many people, crossing a debt off the list that early is what keeps them going.

## What changes if you add $200 a month?

Raising the budget from $900 to $1,100 does more than either choice of method:

- Avalanche: **23 months**, $2,439.49 interest.
- Snowball: **23 months**, $2,728.55 interest.

The extra $200 saves about $800 of interest on the avalanche path and gets you out six months sooner. If you can find money in your budget — for example by [cancelling subscriptions you forgot about](/subscription-finder/) — it usually matters more than the method.

## Which should you choose?

Choose **avalanche** if:

- one of your debts has a much higher rate than the others (credit cards are the usual culprit), and
- you're confident you'll stick with the plan even if the first payoff takes a while.

Choose **snowball** if:

- you've tried to pay down debt before and lost steam, or
- your balances are similar in size, or your rates are close together — then the interest difference is small and the quicker wins are almost free.

A common middle path: pay off any **tiny** balance first for a quick win, then switch to avalanche.

## Things that can beat both methods

- **A lower rate.** A 0% balance transfer or a consolidation loan at a lower rate can cut interest directly. Check the transfer fee, the promotional period and the rate after it ends, and don't run the card back up.
- **Avoiding new debt.** Neither method works if new charges keep landing on the cards you're paying off.
- **Windfalls.** Tax refunds or bonuses applied to the target debt shorten the timeline noticeably.

## Common questions

**Does the snowball method cost a lot more?**
It depends on how different your rates are. In the example above the gap is $375 over two and a half years. With one very high-rate card and a large balance, the gap can be much bigger. Run your own numbers in the planner to see.

**Should I stop saving to pay off debt faster?**
Many people keep a small emergency fund while paying off debt so that a surprise expense doesn't go back on a credit card. How much is right depends on your situation.

**Do I need to tell my lenders which method I'm using?**
No. You simply pay the minimum on each debt and send the extra to your target.

**Is my data saved when I use the planner?**
No. It runs in your browser. The numbers are kept in the page address only so you can bookmark your plan.
