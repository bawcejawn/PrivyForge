iPhones save photos as **HEIC** files by default. They look great and take up less space than JPGs, but plenty of software can't open them: older Windows setups, some Android apps, photo-printing kiosks and many website upload forms. Here are four ways to deal with that, from quickest to most permanent.

## What a HEIC file is

HEIC (High Efficiency Image Container) stores a photo using HEVC compression, the same technology behind H.265 video. Apple switched the iPhone camera to it with iOS 11 because a HEIC photo is much smaller than a JPG of similar quality. The photo itself is fine; the problem is only that some programs can't read the format.

## Way 1: Convert to JPG in your browser (no upload)

The fastest fix for a handful of photos, on any computer:

1. Open the [HEIC to JPG converter](/heic-to-jpg/).
2. Drop in your HEIC photos (as many as you like).
3. Pick a quality (85 looks like the original) and, if you want smaller files, a maximum width.
4. Download each JPG, or all of them as a ZIP.

It works in Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari. The conversion runs on your own device, so the photos are never sent to a server. That matters for photos of documents, kids or your home. In browsers without a built-in HEIC decoder, the page downloads its own decoder once (about 0.8 MB); after that it also works offline.

The converted JPGs also have their **location data (GPS) removed**, which is worth knowing if you're about to post or email them.

## Way 2: Let Windows open HEIC directly

Recent versions of Windows can display HEIC photos in the Photos app once Microsoft's codec packages are installed. Depending on your Windows version and how your PC was set up, you may need the **HEIF Image Extensions** and **HEVC Video Extensions** from the Microsoft Store. Microsoft has charged a small fee for the HEVC package in the past, and some PCs come with it preinstalled. Check the Store listing for the current price.

This is the right choice if you regularly view HEIC photos on that PC. It doesn't help when a website or another program needs a JPG.

## Way 3: Have the iPhone send JPGs when you transfer

If you copy photos to a computer with a cable, the iPhone can convert them for you:

- **Settings → Photos → Transfer to Mac or PC → Automatic** converts photos to a compatible format during the transfer.

The originals on your phone stay HEIC.

## Way 4: Shoot JPG from now on

If compatibility matters more to you than storage space:

- **Settings → Camera → Formats → Most Compatible** makes the camera save new photos as JPG.

The trade-off is that new photos take up noticeably more space on your phone and in iCloud. Photos you've already taken stay HEIC.

## Which should you pick?

| Situation | Best option |
| --- | --- |
| A few photos to email or upload right now | Convert in the browser (Way 1) |
| You view iPhone photos on this PC every week | Windows extensions (Way 2) |
| You back up to a PC with a cable | Automatic transfer (Way 3) |
| You constantly hit "file type not supported" | Shoot Most Compatible (Way 4) |

## Common questions

**Does converting HEIC to JPG lose quality?**
JPG compression is lossy, so technically yes, but at quality 85 or higher the difference is very hard to see. The JPG will usually be larger than the HEIC was.

**What about Live Photos?**
A Live Photo is a still image plus a short video. Converting gives you the still image as a JPG; the motion part isn't included.

**Can Android phones open HEIC?**
Many recent Android phones and gallery apps can display HEIC, but older phones and plenty of apps can't. Converting to JPG is the safe choice when you're not sure what the recipient uses.

**Is it safe to use an online HEIC converter?**
Many online converters upload your photos to their server to do the work. If the photos are private, use a converter that runs in your browser, like the one above, or one of the built-in options on your phone or PC.
